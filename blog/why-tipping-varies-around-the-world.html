Why Tipping Percentages Vary So Much Around the World

Published 2026-09-14

Tipping isn't a universal custom — it's actually unusual

If you've traveled internationally, you may have noticed that the confident "15-20%" tipping rule many people follow at home doesn't translate everywhere. Tipping as a strong social expectation is actually far from universal; it's concentrated heavily in North America, with much of the rest of the world treating it as optional, modest, or in some cases genuinely unwelcome.

Why US tipping culture became so ingrained

US tipping norms trace back partly to a labor law quirk: many US states allow employers to pay tipped workers (like restaurant servers) a lower minimum wage than other jobs, on the assumption that tips will make up the difference. That legal structure, dating back to the late 1800s and early 1900s, is a major reason tipping became functionally mandatory in the US rather than a discretionary bonus for exceptional service, which is closer to how it began.

Where tipping is considered rude, not generous

In Japan, tipping in most restaurants can actually cause confusion or mild offense — excellent service is considered simply part of the job and the price already paid, and leaving extra money can be seen as implying the staff need charity or that they weren't already trying their best. South Korea has historically had similar norms, though this has been slowly shifting in some tourist-facing businesses.

Where a service charge replaces tipping entirely

Much of continental Europe includes a service charge in the listed price or bill by default (sometimes labeled "service compris" in France, for example), making additional tipping optional and typically much smaller than US norms — rounding up or leaving small change is common, rather than calculating a fixed percentage.

Why this matters when traveling

Following your home country's tipping percentage abroad can either underpay expected tips (in some Middle Eastern and Latin American countries where tipping is expected but at different rates) or feel awkward and unnecessary (in much of Asia and parts of Europe) — a quick check of local norms before a trip avoids both problems.
