Why Two Word Counters Give You Different Numbers

Published 2026-09-14

The short answer: there's no single official rule

If you've ever pasted the same paragraph into two different word counters and gotten two different numbers, you're not imagining it — and neither tool is necessarily "wrong." There is no universal standard for what counts as a word, so every tool makes its own small judgment calls, and those calls add up over a long document.

Where the disagreements come from

  • Hyphenated words: is "well-known" one word or two? Some counters split on hyphens, others don't.
  • Numbers and dates: is "2026" a word? Most counters say yes if it's surrounded by spaces, but formatting varies.
  • Contractions: "don't" is usually one word, but a counter that splits on apostrophes might count it as two.
  • Em dashes and slashes: "yes—or no" can be read as two words or three depending on whether the dash counts as a space.
  • Stray whitespace: double spaces, tabs, and trailing line breaks from a copy-paste can occasionally get miscounted by simpler tools.

Which number should you trust?

If you're submitting to a specific platform — a school assignment checked in Microsoft Word, or a job application checked in Google Docs — the number that matters is whichever tool they will actually use to check it, not the "most accurate" one in the abstract. When in doubt, paste your final text into that exact platform yourself before submitting.

For everyday writing — blog posts, emails, social captions — small differences of a handful of words almost never matter, and any reasonably built counter (ours included) will get you close enough to plan your writing. Our Word Counter splits on whitespace, which matches how Word and Google Docs count in the vast majority of real-world text, so it's a solid default when you don't have a specific target tool in mind.

A practical tip for hitting an exact target

If an assignment says "exactly 500 words" or "500-550 words," write to roughly 5% under the upper limit first, then re-check the count in whatever tool your instructor or client will use. Small edits near the end of a draft (cutting a redundant sentence, tightening a phrase) shift word counts more than people expect.
